Why do some bowlers average 20 and others 35?

A bowling average is the number of runs a bowler gives away for each wicket they take, so lower is better. We studied every ball of 898 international Tests to find out what really separates the best bowlers from the rest.

Runs conceded÷Wickets taken=Bowling average Example: a bowler who gives away 3,000 runs while taking 100 wickets averages 30.0. Anything in the low 20s is outstanding.

The short version

  • Much of a bowler's record is luck. Over a bowler's first 100 wickets, about 54% of the difference between averages comes down to chance.
  • Once luck is set aside, the bowler matters most. The bowler's own ability accounts for 86% of the real differences. The conditions they were given add almost nothing to the gaps between bowlers: over 100 wickets the good and bad draws mostly even out, and the bowlers who were dealt harder conditions tended to be the better ones, so conditions narrowed the gaps rather than widened them.
  • Who they bowl at and where matter most. Facing a weak line-up instead of a strong one can move an average by about 10 runs per wicket; for spinners, the country they bowl in matters even more.
  • The skill that lasts is keeping runs down. Bowlers who concede few runs keep doing it. Bursts of extra wickets don't last.

The obvious answer goes round in a circle

Ask why a bowler has a low average and the usual answer is "they take lots of wickets and don't give away many runs". That is true, but it only describes the average. It is like saying a shop makes a profit because its takings are high and its costs are low. We wanted the reasons behind those numbers.

So we asked a different question: how much better or worse was each bowler than an ordinary bowler would have been, bowling the very same balls in the very same situations?

How we tested it

  1. We gathered every ball from international Tests in the public Cricsheet database: 1.5 million balls since 2007, bowled by 692 bowlers.
  2. For every ball, we worked out what an ordinary bowler could expect from it. That depends on who was batting and how good they are, the ground and country, whether it was a home or away Test, the state of the match, the age of the ball, and who was bowling at the other end. We also allowed for who usually bowls in each place, so a country's pitches aren't credited with the skill of its own bowlers.
  3. Adding those up gives each bowler an expected average: what an ordinary bowler would have averaged in their shoes.
  4. The gap between the actual and the expected average is what the bowler did themselves.
  5. To keep it fair, we compared every bowler at the same stage of their career: their first 100 Test wickets. 51 bowlers who made their debut in 2007 or later have reached 100 wickets.
  6. Finally, we split those 100 wickets in half and checked whether a bowler's strengths in the first half showed up again in the second. A real skill should repeat. Luck won't.

Finding 1

Much of a bowler's record is luck

Picture two equally good bowlers. One finds edges that carry to slip. The other sees them fall just short or go through a gap. Over a few series, that kind of luck builds up and can make the two look very different.

Over 100 wickets, about 54% of the variation is chance. That is why a bowler's average over their first 50 wickets says little about their next 50 (carry-over 0.19 on a scale where 1 would mean the same bowler every time).

What this meansBe patient with a bowler's numbers, good or bad. Even 50 wickets is not enough to judge.

Finding 2

Once luck is set aside, the bowler matters most

Once luck is set aside, we split the real differences between bowlers into four parts:

  • Pace or spin. In typical Test conditions, spinners give away about 34.8 runs per wicket and fast bowlers about 30.6.
  • Conditions they were given. Things outside the bowler's control: the strength of the opposition, home or away, the country, the partner at the other end.
  • Match situations. Things the bowler partly creates. A bowler who removes the top order gets to bowl at tail-enders, which is easier.
  • The bowler. Whatever is left once all of the above is accounted for.

The bowler accounts for 86% of the real differences, being a spinner or a fast bowler for 16%, match situations for 2% and the conditions they were given for ≈0%. The conditions they were given add almost nothing to the gaps between bowlers: over 100 wickets the good and bad draws mostly even out, and the bowlers who were dealt harder conditions tended to be the better ones, so conditions narrowed the gaps rather than widened them.

What this meansConditions still move an average ball by ball (next finding), so check them before judging one bowler's record. But across bowlers who reach 100 wickets, the bowler is the main story.

Facing a weak line-up instead of a strong one can move an average by about 10 runs per wicket; for spinners, the country they bowl in matters even more. Bowling at home rather than away is worth about 3.4 runs per wicket. Who bowls at the other end doesn't measurably change a bowler's average.

A few more patterns, each with everything else kept typical:

  • Batter's hand. Spinners do about 10% better when the batter's hand is opposite their bowling arm (a right-arm spinner to a left-hander, or a left-arm spinner to a right-hander).
  • Fourth innings. A spinner who would average 30 in typical conditions averages about 26 in the fourth innings, when the pitch is at its most worn.
  • Bowling arm. Left-arm fast bowlers average about 6% less than right-arm ones, and left- and right-arm spinners average about the same.

Finding 4

The skill that lasts is keeping runs down

We measured two things for every bowler, both compared with what an ordinary bowler would do in the same spots: how few runs they gave away, and how often they took wickets. Then we checked whether each one carried over from the first 50 wickets to the next 50.

Bowlers who give away fewer runs than expected in their first 50 wickets keep doing so in the next 50 (carry-over 0.74). Bowlers who take more wickets than expected early on usually don't (carry-over 0.04). A burst of extra wickets is mostly luck.

That surprised us. On paper, wicket-taking is what makes averages differ the most. But it is the run-saving part that is a dependable trait.

Some habits are very consistent but make no difference to the average. Bowlers who bowl a lot of no-balls and wides keep bowling them (carry-over 0.84), yet it doesn't predict whether their average is good or bad.

What this meansWhen judging a bowler, look at how few runs they give away for the conditions. It is the most reliable sign of quality.

Some low averages owe a lot to the conditions. Sri Lanka's P Jayasuriya averaged 29.8 over his first 100 wickets, well below the 34.9 a spinner averages in typical conditions. But his conditions were easy, mostly because of the batters he bowled at and where he bowled, so an ordinary spinner bowling the same balls would have averaged 27.0. His conditions were worth about 7.8 runs per wicket, and he did 2.7 runs per wicket worse than an ordinary spinner would have in them.

What didn't make a difference

  • Umpires. Umpires who give more lbws in other matches made no measurable difference.
  • Era. Averages in typical conditions changed by a few percent at most between 2007 and 2026.
  • Special talents that don't repeat. Being unusually good at taking wickets against new batters, with the new ball, against the top order or late in a spell did not carry over from one half to the next. Only wicket-taking at home and after three dot balls in a row carried over at all, and much less than keeping runs down.
  • Background. A bowler's type, age at debut and one-day international record before their Test debut could not reliably predict how they would do later.

What we can't tell you

  • How they do it. The data records what happened on each ball, not the speed, swing, spin or length of the delivery. So we can say a bowler is good at keeping runs down, but not why.
  • Only bowlers who lasted. We studied bowlers who reached 100 Test wickets. Bowlers who struggled early and were dropped are not included, so this describes good bowlers, not all bowlers.
  • A small group. 51 bowlers is not many, so the smaller effects here are uncertain.
  • Some situations are partly the bowler's doing. Bowling at tail-enders is easier, but a bowler often gets there by taking early wickets. We kept these situations separate so they are not mistaken for luck.
  • Missing matches. 19 of the 815 Tests since 2007 aren't in the public data (mostly Afghanistan's), so a few bowlers have some wickets skipped.

Data: ball-by-ball records of international men's Tests from Cricsheet (cricsheet.org), 2001 to 2026; bowlers whose first Test is in 2007 or later. The full technical analysis is in the report "Bowler Average Factors".
