What actually makes a Test bowling average low

An average is runs ÷ wickets, so "they take wickets often" and "they get batters out before they're set" only restate it. This analysis prices every delivery's conditions using every other bowler, then asks what is left and whether it repeats.

Where the real spread comes from

Each bowler's average is split exactly into a baseline for their type, the contribution of each group of conditions (its grouped Shapley value, in runs per wicket), and what is left: the bowler effect. The chart shows how much of the real, noise-free spread each part accounts for.

Dealt conditions are fixed before the ball is bowled and outside the bowler's control. Shaped situations partly result from the bowler's or team's own success: remove the top order and you bowl at the tail; bowl well and the captain keeps you on. They are reported separately and never counted as luck.

The bowler's part: what repeats

Every skill measure here is context-adjusted: actual ÷ what the conditions predict, on a log scale, so +0.10 means about 10% better than expected. Each was measured separately on wickets and wickets . A real trait shows up in both halves (reliability). A useful one also predicts how far the bowler beats the conditions in the second half (prediction). The halves share no deliveries, so neither test can be circular.

Spearman ρ with 95% bootstrap interval. Violet: interval excludes zero.

Why this flips the old conclusion. The earlier report found that strike rate explains about 70% of the variance in averages and economy about 40%, and concluded that wicket-taking "does most of the work". That is true of the observed spread, but over -wicket halves most of the wicket-taking spread is luck. The part of a bowler that carries forward is how few runs they concede for the conditions.

What doesn't matter

  • Situational "skills" don't repeat. Being unusually good against new batters, with the new ball, against the top order, late in a spell, at home, or after three dot balls in a row all have reliability close to zero between halves.
  • Consistent style, no effect. Wides and no-balls (reliability ), share bowled or lbw () and share caught by the keeper () are stable traits of a bowler, but none of them predicts their average.
  • Umpires. Umpires' LBW rates in other matches make no measurable difference.
  • Bowling arm, beyond a small edge for left-arm pace, and era within 2007–2026 move averages by a few percent at most.
  • Profile alone. Type, arm, age at debut, debut year and white-ball experience together predict little of the second-half average (leave-one-out R² ; the noise ceiling is ).

How it works

Rules that keep it non-circular

  • Conditions are priced from everyone else. Two additive log-link models, for the chance of a wicket and the expected runs on each delivery, are trained on every Test delivery since 2007. They are cross-fitted, so a bowler's own balls are always scored by models that never saw that bowler. Inputs are only things known before the ball is bowled.
  • Skill claims must survive out of sample (the split-half test above).
  • Strike rate, economy, dots and maidens are never inputs.

What each delivery knows

The batter's pre-match Test average, strike rate and experience, their position, hand and balls faced so far; the opposition's top-seven pre-match average; overs since the new ball; innings and overs into the match; the lead and wickets down; host country (from the series name, e.g. "India tour of Australia") and home, away or neutral; toss; era; DRS; the umpires' LBW rate in other matches; opening the bowling, over within the spell, spell number and overs already bowled; and the pre-match average of the bowler at the other end.

Attribution

xAvg = expected runs ÷ expected wickets over the bowler's window. A group's contribution is its grouped Shapley value: the average change in xAvg from switching that group from typical to actual conditions, over every order of switching the others. "Typical" means a random league delivery by the same type of bowler. Because the models are log-linear, that expectation is computed exactly rather than by sampling. Each bowler's parts add up to their actual average (asserted in the notebook).

The additive model predicts as well as gradient boosting (wicket log-loss vs ), so little is lost by keeping it additive. Over all deliveries, predicted wickets and runs are within 0.5% of actual.

Caveats

  • No ball-tracking. Pace, swing, seam movement, line and length aren't in Cricsheet, so the bowler effect is "everything about the bowler", not a breakdown of technique.
  • Selection into the window. Only bowlers who reached Test wickets are studied, and poor starters are dropped before they get there. This narrows the range of skill, and that is part of why luck looms so large.
  • Shaped situations are a mix. Bowling at the tail or to new batters is partly the bowler's doing, partly teammates'. Treat that share as "not luck, not purely the bowler".
  • Approximations. The second new ball is assumed taken at 80 overs. Spells break after a gap of more than one over by that bowler, so drinks and session breaks aren't detected. Batter records only count Tests in the data, so careers that began before 2001 are undercounted. Wicketkeepers are inferred from stumpings.
  • Coverage. Cricsheet misses some Tests; only bowlers whose first Test in the data is 2007 or later are used, so wicket 1 really is their first.
