Why do some bowlers average 20 and others 35?

A bowling average is the number of runs a bowler gives away for each wicket they take, so lower is better. We studied every ball of {{n_tests}} international Tests to find out what really separates the best bowlers from the rest.

Runs conceded÷Wickets taken=Bowling average Example: a bowler who gives away 1,500 runs while taking 50 wickets averages 30.0. Anything in the low 20s is outstanding.

The short version

  • Early figures are mostly luck. Over a bowler's first 50 wickets, about {{luck50}} of the difference between averages comes down to chance.
  • Conditions matter a lot at first. Where a bowler plays, who they bowl at and who bowls at the other end shape an early average more than the bowler does.
  • Over a longer stretch, the bowler matters most. By 100 wickets, the bowler's own ability is the biggest single factor.
  • The skill that lasts is keeping runs down. Bowlers who concede few runs keep doing it. Bursts of extra wickets don't last.

The obvious answer goes round in a circle

Ask why a bowler has a low average and the usual answer is "they take lots of wickets and don't give away many runs". That is true, but it only describes the average. It is like saying a shop makes a profit because its takings are high and its costs are low. We wanted the reasons behind those numbers.

So we asked a different question: how much better or worse was each bowler than an ordinary bowler would have been, bowling the very same balls in the very same situations?

How we tested it

  1. We gathered every ball from international Tests in the public Cricsheet database: {{n_balls}} balls since 2007, bowled by {{n_bowlers_model}} bowlers.
  2. For every ball, we worked out what an ordinary bowler could expect from it. That depends on who was batting and how good they are, the ground and country, whether it was a home or away Test, the state of the match, the age of the ball, and who was bowling at the other end.
  3. Adding those up gives each bowler an expected average: what an ordinary bowler would have averaged in their shoes.
  4. The gap between the actual and the expected average is what the bowler did themselves.
  5. To keep it fair, we compared every bowler at the same stage of their career: their first 50 Test wickets ({{n50}} bowlers) and their first 100 ({{n100}} bowlers).
  6. Finally, we split each stretch in half and checked whether a bowler's strengths in the first half showed up again in the second. A real skill should repeat. Luck won't.

Finding 1

Much of a bowler's early record is luck

Picture two equally good bowlers. One finds edges that carry to slip. The other sees them fall just short or go through a gap. Over a few series, that kind of luck builds up and can make the two look very different.

{{luck_chart}}

Over 50 wickets, about {{luck50}} of the variation is chance. Even over 100 wickets it is still {{luck100}}. That is why a bowler's average over their first 25 wickets says very little about their next 25.

What this meansBe patient with a young bowler's early numbers, good or bad. A couple of series is not enough to judge.

Finding 2

Early on, conditions matter most. Over time, the bowler does.

Once luck is set aside, we split the real differences between bowlers into four parts:

  • Pace or spin. In typical Test conditions, spinners give away about {{spin_base}} runs per wicket and fast bowlers about {{pace_base}}.
  • Conditions they were given. Things outside the bowler's control: the strength of the opposition, home or away, the country, the partner at the other end.
  • Match situations. Things the bowler partly creates. A bowler who removes the top order gets to bowl at tail-enders, which is easier.
  • The bowler. Whatever is left once all of the above is accounted for.

Over the first 50 wickets, the conditions a bowler was given ({{dealt50}}) count for about twice as much as the bowler ({{bowler50}}). Over 100 wickets it flips: the bowler accounts for {{bowler100}} and conditions for {{dealt100}}. A longer career spreads a bowler across more grounds and opponents, so the luck of the draw evens out.

One thing to keep in mind: only bowlers who were good enough to keep their place reach 100 wickets, so the two groups are not identical. Part of the change may come from that.

What this meansBefore praising or blaming a bowler's early average, ask where they played and who they bowled at.

The opposition's batting matters most. Facing one of the weakest line-ups instead of one of the strongest can be the difference between a bowler averaging in the low 20s and the mid 30s. Home advantage is worth a few runs per wicket, and so is having a good bowler at the other end.

A few more patterns: off-spinners do about {{offspin_lhb}} better against left-handed batters, and spinners are about {{spin4th}} cheaper in the fourth innings, when the pitch is at its most worn.

Finding 4

The skill that lasts is keeping runs down

We measured two things for every bowler, both compared with what an ordinary bowler would do in the same spots: how few runs they gave away, and how often they took wickets. Then we checked whether each one carried over from the first half of their stretch to the second.

Bowlers who give away fewer runs than expected early on keep doing so (carry-over {{run50}} and {{run100}}). Bowlers who take more wickets than expected early on usually don't ({{wkt50}}, and {{wkt100}} over 100 wickets). A burst of extra wickets is mostly luck.

That surprised us. On paper, wicket-taking is what makes averages differ the most. But it is the run-saving part that is a dependable trait.

Some habits are very consistent but make no difference to the average. Bowlers who bowl a lot of no-balls and wides keep bowling them (carry-over {{noball100}}), yet it doesn't predict whether their average is good or bad.

What this meansWhen judging a bowler, look at how few runs they give away for the conditions. It is the most reliable sign of quality.

What didn't make a difference

  • Umpires. Umpires who give more lbws in other matches made no measurable difference.
  • Era. Averages in typical conditions changed little between 2007 and 2026.
  • Left arm or right arm. Left-arm fast bowlers have a small edge, but arm makes little difference overall.
  • Special talents that don't repeat. Being unusually good with the new ball, against new batters, against the top order, at home, or late in a spell did not carry over from one half of a career stretch to the next.
  • Background. A bowler's type, age at debut and one-day international record before their Test debut could not reliably predict how they would do later.

What we can't tell you

  • How they do it. The data records what happened on each ball, not the speed, swing, spin or length of the delivery. So we can say a bowler is good at keeping runs down, but not why.
  • Only bowlers who lasted. We studied bowlers who reached 50 or 100 Test wickets. Bowlers who struggled early and were dropped are not included.
  • Small groups. {{n100}} bowlers reached 100 wickets with a debut in 2007 or later, so the 100-wicket results are less certain than the 50-wicket ones.
  • Some situations are partly the bowler's doing. Bowling at tail-enders is easier, but a bowler often gets there by taking early wickets. We kept these situations separate so they are not mistaken for luck.

Data: ball-by-ball records of international men's Tests from Cricsheet (cricsheet.org), 2001 to 2026; bowlers whose first Test is in 2007 or later. The full technical analysis is in the reports "Bowler Average Factors - First 50" and "Bowler Average Factors - First 100".
